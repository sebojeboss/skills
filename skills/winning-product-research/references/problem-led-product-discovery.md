# Discover products from actual problems

## When to read

The starting point is a frustration, workaround, homemade solution or buying history rather than a catalog item.

## How to do the work

Locate people describing actual attempts in relevant public communities, reviews or supplied evidence. Search generic task language, failed alternatives and constraints, paired with words that only appear once something happened: "stopped using", "returned", "switched from", "still using", "bought another". Include satisfied-alternative searches, because the cheap workaround that works is the competitor you most need to know about. Read the opening account, parent context and consequential replies/followups. Distinguish an owner, interested observer, maker and seller; several reposts of one story do not establish prevalence.

Recover the sequence: what changed, what they tried, where it failed, what they considered, what they rejected and what they accepted instead. Keep the sacrificed benefit. Someone can reject a more powerful product because it adds setup, blocks another essential activity or costs too much. Features that seem missing may be the reason an item fits: someone who keeps getting pulled into notifications whenever they use a phone timer may want a single-purpose kitchen timer precisely because it can't do anything else.

Translate the unresolved constraint into a function to search, not a finished persona. “Needs small tools visible without using desk space” leads to shallow wall storage and hanging strips. It does not establish a universal audience of disorganized people. Compare product candidates with the existing workaround and doing nothing. Satisfaction with a cheaper alternative is useful contrary evidence.

Private customer evidence can inform local reasoning, but do not send its distinctive context, identifiers or paraphrases to external tools. Derive independent generic public searches; if a needed inquiry cannot be made without private disclosure, keep the work local or obtain narrowly scoped permission for the actual recipient/data. Public groups do not grant access to private groups or authorize outreach.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: a renter repeatedly loses small tools but rejects closed boxes because tools disappear from view. A wall rack solves visibility but needs drilling. A freestanding shallow tray sacrifices desk area yet respects the rental constraint. Nominate both with the constraint attached; investigate dimensions and stable placement before making fit claims. A user with wall space may rationally reverse the choice.

Illustrative: a buyer stops using a useful item after their routine changes. That can reveal a narrower use window without indicating a product defect. Do not force a subscription or repeat-purchase story onto a need that ends.

## Deliverable and checks

Write a problem-to-candidate map: actual event, attempted alternatives, rejection reason, accepted sacrifice, product function, evidence, contrary account and unknown. A request to buy is not a completed purchase; one detailed story is not a demand estimate. Health-related anecdotes do not substantiate product efficacy. Stop once concrete constraints nominate candidates or the decisive question needs new firsthand evidence. Deep source collection belongs to customer research; no fixed interview quota is required for a narrow decision.
