# Discover from marketplace signals

## When to read

Start from a marketplace search, best-seller list, listing reviews, rank or order displays, or a tool's apparent momentum. Use these to form candidates and buyer questions.

## How to do the work

Search a category or use term in the relevant locale, and sort by sales or best-seller rank where the marketplace allows it. Open the listing and capture the catalog ID, seller, selected variant, price and currency, quantity and delivery context. Confirm which configuration the pictures and specifications describe. A cheap accessory variant can explain the headline price, and a review total can pool several variants.

Read reviews for decisions, not stars. Look for what the buyer used before, why they chose this, how they actually use it, returns, and recommendations that changed over time. Favorable and unfavorable accounts both matter. Read enough context to avoid turning "wanted this to work" into successful use. Reviews on an adjacent listing can reveal constraints worth checking, but a larger review count doesn't make that listing interchangeable with yours.

Keep native signals and vendor estimates apart. For every number, record the provider, time window, currency or units, what it measures and when you saw it. A rank is not a sales count, and a seller's claim is not an independent order record. If you can't find a definition, say so rather than comparing incompatible totals. A listing with few recorded orders supplies less purchase history within that listing’s scope. Neither a low nor a high order count establishes competition in the category or destination channel; inspect other sellers and the channel before judging crowding.

## Moving a product to another channel

Bringing a marketplace seller to short-form video or paid social is a real discovery route, but check the destination first. Search the destination channel for the product before assuming you're first. Sparse results there can mean an opening, different buying behavior, poor economics or simply a bad search, and searching alone won't tell you which. Inventory the available demonstrations and their rights status. Plentiful marketplace photos are not usable video. If no transferable content exists, you'll be inventing every demonstration, which suits an operator who already makes original content better than a beginner.

An old product resurfacing can be worth investigating. Ask what changed, whether use, audience, season, offer, distribution or execution. If nothing changed, a new screenshot of the same chart adds little.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: a tool shows rising sales for a portable craft device, there are many marketplace demonstrations, and few ads appear in the proposed market. Shortlist it, then check power compatibility, consumables, output quality and whether the demonstrations can be recreated. Those open conditions can outweigh the chart. An alternative with fewer demonstrations may suit the user's own craft skills better.

Illustrative: one listing has many reviews but mixes two sizes. A small listing describes the exact intended size and includes a complaint that it doesn't fit a standard cabinet. Prefer the exact-fit evidence for that question; don't rank on review count.

## Deliverable and checks

Return the listing identity, variant and review scope, metrics with their definitions, buyer alternatives, content constraints and the destination-channel search. Existing paid tools can speed discovery; check access, credit cost and export rights first. Without them, use public pages or supplied exports. Don't install a review-export extension or upload a private brief just to avoid manual reading. Stop when the next uncertainty needs exact-item evidence or an authorized commercial test.
