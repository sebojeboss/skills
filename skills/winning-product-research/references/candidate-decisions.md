# Decide what to pursue

## When to read

Compare a shortlist, read early test results, interpret saturation, or revisit a failed attempt. Keep each decision attached to an operating configuration, not an abstract product.

## Settle the commitment-changing fact first

Before more traffic, ask what single fact could make the offer impossible regardless of demand. Usually it's one of these: the supplier needs a batch order you can't fund, the item can't be sourced through anything you have access to, the claim the angle depends on isn't supported for this SKU, the packaging won't survive the route, or a seasonal date will pass before deliveries arrive. If one of those is open, resolve it before buying more clicks. More carts can't fix an order you can't fulfill, and stopping a test because supply collapsed is not evidence that buyers didn't want it.

Then work out what has to be true for the offer to work: the buyer's situation and current alternative, what this exact product contributes, how you'll reach those buyers, and what you have to supply (footage, supplier access, cash, support time). Separate an observed problem from a persuasive story about it. A more specific angle is not automatically a truer claim.

## Compare opportunity costs, not unrelated scores

Preserve the user's supplied cost basis. If they give a landed cost, identify what it includes before adding freight again; do not relabel it as product cost or a pre-shipping figure. Price minus stated landed cost is the remainder before unspecified costs, not established contribution or profit. Calculate that remainder when useful, name only the missing costs that matter to the decision, and rank the next investigation without inventing demand or sales.

The real question is what this candidate costs you in money, filming time and attention that a better-evidenced one could have used. A product with plenty of content may need a filming skill you don't have. A less novel item with clear supply and a reachable use case may be the better next bet. You give up some novelty upside for an investigation you can actually run. Change the comparison when production access or supplier terms change.

Look for **room for error**: early unit economics comfortable enough to survive the cost per order rising as spend grows. A product that only just breaks even at test scale can easily slip into a loss as spend rises, so plan for that rather than hope against it. Price the next learning step rather than persistence itself. Repeating the same configuration isn't learning; a retest needs a named hypothesis and a cost you can afford.

When you break your own criteria on purpose, for example running a thin-margin product with no repeat potential because the momentum is obvious, write down which rule you're breaking, what you need to see, and by when. That's a deliberate bet you can review later. Breaking a rule without noticing is drift.

## Read early tests against each other

A small budget forces a ranking, not a verdict. When products run side by side, ask which deserves the next dollar:

- Steady daily carts and orders beat a better click-through rate with no carts.
- Expensive clicks that never reach the cart are worse than either. One favorable metric doesn't outweigh traffic that costs too much and goes nowhere.
- A "purchase" in the ad dashboard with no matching store order isn't a sale until you've reconciled it. A misfiring pixel is one common explanation. Duplicate events, attribution timing, and test, cancelled or pending orders are others. Check store, payment and refund records before counting it.
- Scattered spikes are weaker than consistency. A single large order can make a short test look profitable.

Cutting the weaker product is a budget decision: say so, and say what would reopen it, such as a better demonstration, cheaper traffic or a repaired measurement. Sometimes the right call is to decline an extra experiment because it could eat the money the leading product needs. Fast cutting saves cash and also discards some products that would have worked with patience. Keep that tradeoff in view rather than adopting someone else's kill number.

## Name the failure precisely

"It didn't work" hides several different failures, and each points to a different next move:

- **Orders came, but each one lost money.** The price buyers accept leaves no room for acquisition cost. More volume makes it worse. Change the offer or price, or drop it.
- **No usable content.** This is an operator problem, not a demand problem; the product may suit someone who can film it.
- **No clear buyer.** Targeting and copy had no anchor. Go back to buyer research before more spend.
- **Ran out of calendar.** A seasonal item whose deliveries would arrive late is finished for this season, not disproven.
- **Broke in transit, or a premium feature didn't travel.** The product the buyer receives includes its packaging. Fragile "premium" presentation can raise price and breakage at once.
- **Lost interest.** A legitimate reason to stop, and it says nothing about demand.
- **The numbers were wrong.** Ads left running, costs missing, profit defined differently. Rebuild the books before concluding anything.
- **Scaled and then lost money.** Early profit evaporated as spend rose, or returns arrived later. The test result changed after the test.

## Saturation, age and freshness

"Saturated" needs scope: which market, channel, offer and date? Sparse visible competitors can mean opportunity, poor economics, buyers on another channel, or an incomplete search. Old products regain relevance when the use, audience, season or offer changes. Fresh ads can be brief experiments.

Choose freshness when investigating early entry and persistence when studying an enduring message. Both are publication signals, not spend evidence. Use the pruning check in [ad-library discovery](ad-library-discovery.md#read-the-inventory-for-what-to-inspect-next) before treating a fresh ad as a surviving test.

## Dispositions

- **Discovered:** a lead with an origin. Unchosen is not failed.
- **Investigating:** a named uncertainty is being resolved.
- **Deferred:** the next commitment depends on missing evidence or capacity. Name it.
- **Rejected for this configuration:** known facts defeat the promise or the acceptable commitment. Rejecting an angle need not reject the product, and rejecting a supplier need not reject either.
- **Advance to a named check:** sample review, an exact supplier quote, a buyer inquiry or another bounded step. Not launch authorization.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative supplied facts: two products share a $400 test. After four days, one has eleven carts and a few orders every day. The other has one cart, a higher cost per click, and three "purchases" in the ad dashboard that never appear in the store. Reconcile those three events against store and payment records first. Whatever caused them, the cart gap already justifies moving the remaining budget to the first product. Record what would justify retesting the second: a demonstration that earns cheaper clicks, or a repaired measurement showing real orders.

Illustrative: two products both lose money early. One can't arrive before the seasonal event; the other had a broken checkout. Stop the first and repair the second. That isn't inconsistent; the evidence differs. Before proposing another attempt, state what it could tell apart and how much loss remains affordable.

Illustrative: an operator's rule is "nothing priced under $30, because cheap items can't carry acquisition cost." A $15 kitchen gadget keeps appearing in their discovery feed with buyers asking where to get it. They test it anyway, as a bundle of two to lift the order value. They write down the broken rule and set a check on contribution after ad spend and product cost at a fixed spend. If contribution stays thin, the rule was right. If it holds, they record the exception and the condition that made it work (the bundle) rather than quietly dropping the rule.

## Deliverable and checks

Write the decision, its support, the contrary account, the sacrifice, the unresolved dependency and the smallest next evidence. Calculate on the price actually collected after discounts, product and packed shipping, fees and a returns allowance, and only with supported numbers; a missing cost is not zero. Contribution before acquisition is not net profit. A subscription enrollment is not a collected renewal, and the refill interval has to match how fast the product is used.
