# Incumbent capabilities and adaptation

## When to read

Load when the user wants to copy a successful competitor, asks what protects an incumbent, or needs to decide what to adapt.

## Map what the visible choice depends on

Every visible choice sits on something you can't see. For each one worth adapting, ask who produces the demonstration, what exact item can be supplied, when money leaves and arrives, who trusts the promise, and what obligation follows the sale. A mature brand can take preorders because repeat customers trust its dates. The button can be reproduced; the history can't. Paying a supplier more per unit for later payment terms, launching new colors to an existing collector base, or pausing a marketplace channel because direct demand already uses all the stock are each enabled by things an entrant doesn't have yet. The visible page doesn't show those conditions.

Separate "can't do this now" from "can't ever do this." Missing supplier access, creative throughput, account history or customers can be built. Absence of a marketplace match doesn't prove the incumbent has an exclusive, and original creative doesn't keep copycats away for long. An incumbent with real original work can still see results slide when copies flood in, and keep selling organically after paid ads stop working.

## Why straight copies fail

Copying an ad copies its hook, not the reasons buyers trusted the seller. A pattern worth expecting from straight copies is interest that dies at checkout: carts, even checkouts, and no purchases, because the product looked good and the store didn't. Several other things usually change at the same time, such as the store, the audience a promotion reaches, the footage available, and the channel. So a failed copy rarely tells you which part failed. Reusing someone's ad, product photos or store also creates rights problems that originality would have avoided.

If you adapt, change what the competitor's buyers would notice:

- **The offer.** A cheap add-on from the same supplier can raise perceived value for very little and still ship in one parcel. An add-on from a different supplier can mean two deliveries and two return problems. When one bundled item becomes unavailable or is returned, decide what happens to the other.
- **The proof.** Use real demonstrations, honest guarantees the supplier can actually back, and reviews that are genuinely yours.
- **The demonstration.** Choose one you can film yourself, repeatedly. A toy may look spectacular only after many skilled attempts, and a public-performance concept needs a willing creator and location. Do the production breakdown on paper first.

If the competitor's best product can't be sourced through anything you have access to, don't build a store around its promise. Choose the nearest offer you can fulfill and say what you gave up.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: a reference store sells a personalized set with a short dispatch promise. Image search finds a similar generic item but no reliable personalization route. The productive response is to keep the buyer problem, investigate exact supply, or choose a supportable offer, not to publish the same promise. Conversely, an old, widely available product can still suit a new audience; age alone doesn't reject it.

Illustrative supplied facts: an entrant's version of a competitor's gadget sells at $19.99 with $6 of ad cost per order and loses money after product and shipping. The same supplier offers a matching accessory for $1.20 more. Bundling the two at $24.99 changes the arithmetic. Recalculate contribution with the real shipping cost, and treat the result as a new configuration to test, not a proven fix.

When a revision changes creative, gift and shipping together, list every change before discussing the response. A later sale can't isolate the gift's contribution. Turn the lesson into a proposed test or revised brief, never an invented replication result.

## Deliverable and checks

Return each visible choice labeled adapt now, verify prerequisite first, or don't copy under present constraints, and say what would change each label. Keep the user's settled direction where the evidence allows. Don't turn every imitation question into a compulsory product search or a moral lecture. Original implementation and licensed assets remain the production boundary.
