# Creative capture and families

## When to read

Load when asked what a competitor's ads say or show, which buying reason they argue, how variants differ, or what changed between captures.

## Start with a meaningful pair

Don't catalog every ad. Pick a contrast that isolates something: same product with a different angle, same opening with a different offer, or same claim with different proof. A pair isn't a controlled performance test, but it shows you what the advertiser is varying.

Break each ad down by job: the opening that earns attention, the problem or situation, what's demonstrated, the proof, the offer, and the action requested. Record the words and visible action behind each interpretation. Keep the headline printed outside the media separate from text inside it. For a carousel, keep the card order and any destination differences. For video, say exactly which intervals you played and which you only read in captions.

## Read the angle, then the angles left unused

An **angle** is the reason to buy that the ad argues for a particular situation. The hook is just how it opens. Once you've identified the incumbent's angle, list the other plausible reasons someone in a different situation would want the same product, and check what each one would require the product to do. Often the competitor has taken the broad reason, and a narrower situation the product truly serves is open. Before handing that gap to creative work, check two things. First, silence in competitor copy doesn't prove no one else uses the angle. Second, the new angle's implied claim has to be supportable for the exact SKU. A specific, vivid angle can still be a false one.

Notice the format choices too. Creative that looks like an ordinary person's post (often called native-looking) is a deliberate choice, and it depends on someone credible being willing to film it. Three cuts of one video with different first lines are one argument tested three times. If the body never changes, the advertiser isn't testing different reasons to buy.

## Families and identity

A **creative family** is your grouping of ads that share an opening, footage or argument. Keep every ad ID and date inside it and state the grouping criterion. Similar text or reused footage suggests a family. Only identical files establish a file match, and a file match still isn't the same ad. A matching opening with a different product quantity is a different offer. Re-encoding or cropping can stop hashes matching on visually identical media, so a hash mismatch isn't evidence of a fresh concept.

Captions establish wording, not who is speaking, delivery, pacing or visual proof, and a loaded thumbnail isn't a viewed video. When captions exist, follow the [caption-reading reference](caption-normalization-contract.md). Read an opening together with its continuation, because the next sentence can qualify a provocative claim. Keep spoken text, screen text and action in separate columns. If the video won't play, keep the copy and thumbnail observations and leave motion and voice unknown. A familiar or repeated presenter doesn't establish identity, synthetic origin or permission to imitate.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: three ad IDs share the same opening demonstration but show different bundles and landing URLs. Group the opening and keep the three offers separate. The useful observation is that the advertiser is varying the offer around a fixed opening, so the offers are what to inspect. It says nothing about spend.

Illustrative: a competitor's ads for a zip cable pouch all argue a tidier desk. A travel forum shows owners buying the same kind of pouch to stop chargers tangling in carry-on bags. That's a candidate angle for a different situation. Before it goes to creative, check whether this pouch's size and padding actually suit a packed bag, and whether other sellers already make that argument.

## Deliverable and checks

Deliver a table of ad ID, observed opening, angle, proof type, offer, CTA and destination, and the portion inspected. Add one useful implication: a claim that needs evidence, an opening that depends on production skill, an unused angle worth checking, or a landing page that resolves an objection. Send an original creative brief to production when requested; don't hand over protected assets as a reusable pack. An observation can identify an effective-looking mechanism without claiming measured effectiveness.
