# Human capture and performance

## When to read

Use for filming directions, a script that is difficult to say, awkward raw footage, or new capture needed during an edit.

## How to do the work

Read the line as something said to one listener who does not already know the product. Identify its thought, not just its word count. Mark where the idea turns, which word carries the distinction and what the listener needs to see. If the line contains several nested qualifications, split it at a meaningful boundary. Preserve fixed claims and selected opening language; do not silently shorten away a limitation or change the message to fit a time target.

A teleprompter supports memory, not delivery. Place it near the camera, adjust scroll speed to the person's phrasing and allow their gaze to return naturally. If eyes visibly chase the text, try a shorter phrase or slower scroll before demanding more energy. Emphasize the new information, not every adjective. Conversational delivery can be deliberate; unscripted stumbling is not the only route to naturalness.

Give the performer a concrete action and listener relationship: explain a control to somebody using it for the first time, or show the feature that changed your choice when that account is true. Avoid notes such as “be more authentic” without locating what feels wrong. Is the rhythm evenly stressed? Does the gaze leave at the key claim? Does the sentence end before the hand reaches the object? Each needs a different correction.

For a stumble, keep recording and restart the complete thought with a small handle before and after it. The editor can often remove the failed phrase. But if the physical action cannot reset invisibly—liquid already poured, packaging already opened—speech and action may need separate takes or voiceover. Keep the change in object state consistent with the edit. Do not ask a creator to repeat a nonrepeatable action merely to obtain a cleaner sentence without planning replacement coverage.

Choose camera support from the handling requirements. Handheld presentation uses a hand. A two-handed adjustment usually needs a supported camera or another operator. Match wardrobe, prop state, camera height and lighting across pickups when they must intercut. Record a short sound check before a long batch: clothing noise, bracelets, room reflections and inconsistent distance can make otherwise usable performance difficult to join. Capture product sounds intentionally if the action depends on hearing a click; do not promise the click proves a seal or mechanical rating.

A-roll is the main performance. B-roll should reveal what the speech cannot efficiently describe or provide relevant context. Capture enough of the action before and after the intended cut to allow the editor to choose a readable join. A beautiful close-up that excludes the operation can be a useful insert but cannot replace missing demonstration coverage.

Review raw footage before anyone edits it. Are all the clips and voiceover lines there, with enough variety to cut an ad from? Do they follow the reference you linked? Is the product used the right way, in a setting that suits the concept? A cluttered background is a problem when it pulls focus from the product or contradicts the premise, such as a calm morning routine; for an honest, lived-in review it can be right. Does the read sound like speech, or like someone reading, with flat stress and eyes tracking the prompter? Is the brand name pronounced the way the brand says it? A smudged lens, shake, a fan or air conditioner under the voice, and phone HDR that will shift color in the edit are all cheaper to catch now than after a client sees the cut. Some noise can be cleaned up. A smudge across the product or a missing action usually means asking for the shot again.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative scenario: a creator must assemble a small stand while delivering a long introduction. Both hands are occupied and looking at the camera causes alignment mistakes. Three workable options serve different purposes: split the speech into short action-linked phrases; record the action steadily and add voiceover; or retain a short direct-to-camera introduction before a separate assembly shot. Voiceover sacrifices continuous personal address but frees attention for the task. A continuous take is appropriate only if the person can perform both clearly and the continuity itself matters.

Illustrative pickup: one correct sentence exists, but its last word sounds swallowed. Request that sentence with neighboring context and matching mic distance, rather than refilm the whole piece. If the background and lighting cannot be matched, use a planned product insert during the join—but listen for the change in room tone. A visual cover does not repair an audible seam.

## Deliverable and checks

Return actionable filming notes: exact thought/action, frame, sound setup, reset state and required handles. For a repair, identify the source time and what existing material remains usable. Inspect the attempted take when available. Do not claim a performance improved from script text alone.
