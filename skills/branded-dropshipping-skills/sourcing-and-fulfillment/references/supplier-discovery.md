# Supplier discovery

## When to read

A product link or image has no supplier, search results are irrelevant, you are choosing between an RFQ and manual search, or the user asks whether two listings identify the same product or factory.

## How to do the work

Search the product, not its marketing name. Build the query from the noun, material, dimensions or capacity, power or plug where relevant, pack count and destination. Keep essential attributes separate from preferences.

When results come back off-category, change the vocabulary instead of piling filters onto a bad query. Try a regional synonym, the trade term or the marketplace's native-language noun. A word that means a plumbing fitting in one country can mean a tool in another. Platform filters can also drop miscategorized listings, so put the decisive attribute in the query text and check that relevance actually improved.

**Choose the route by who pays the search cost.** A request for quotation works when the item is a recognizable, common design: suppliers searching requests find it, and replies arrive quickly. For an unusual item, replies are slow and often off-target, and triaging them can cost more than searching yourself. Either way a reply is a lead, not a selection. Ask for a truthful trial quantity, with any future volumes clearly labelled as scenarios. Inflating volume to attract replies gets you prices for an order you will not place.

**Read listings, not thumbnails.** Open the exact listing, select the actual variant, and see what changes in price, availability and shipping. Capacity and size variants often share one photo. When you ask a question, send it from the listing chat, so it is attached to that item and variant. "Price?" wastes the moment when hidden capacity, material and packing differences surface. Ask for packed gross weight when freight depends on it. Record a stable public locator and the observation date.

**Image matching is candidate generation.** Compare silhouettes, seams, connectors, controls and included accessories locally first. Upload only an image whose transmission is authorized. Several matches may be traders using the same photo, products from different runs, or different specifications. A photo match is a discovery result, not a source-identity finding.

Platform badges measure different things: years of membership, a verified merchant, a factory label. None establishes production capability or batch quality, and a capable factory can have a thin storefront. Where a badge and the profile disagree with the claimed production setup, investigate the contradiction rather than counting badges.

**Match the supplier's role to the service you need.** A trader that consolidates single parcels can fit better than a factory offering only cartons. Some sellers are better served by a domestic importer at a lower margin, trading unit margin for less coordination and capital exposure.

Stop when the remaining uncertainty needs a real quote or sample rather than more lookalike listings. A failed search is a search result, not proof that the product cannot be sourced.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: a regional noun returns tools instead of plumbing parts. A synonym repairs discovery, but the lowest-price card turns out to be a smaller variant. Show the query revision, why that candidate was rejected, and the dispatch question still unanswered. Image similarity cannot settle either the variant or the manufacturer.

## Deliverable and checks

A supplier-candidate table with:

- public locator;
- observed SKU or variant;
- fit and deviations;
- role claim;
- evidence date;
- destination and dispatch unknowns;
- the next discriminating check.

Keep rejected candidates, with the reason, when it helps the choice. Do not present an uncontacted listing as a firm offer.
