# Stock and dispatch

## When to read

You are assessing availability, a supplier says "in stock", delivery copy needs an evidence basis, or stock reservation or prebuying is being considered.

## How to do the work

**A stock count is not a claim.** Sixty units in a supplier's warehouse and six sales a week look like ten weeks of cover, but you have no claim on those units. Every other store selling the product draws from the same shelf. Ask what the count means:

- saleable on hand;
- upstream availability;
- shared stock;
- stock allocated to you;
- replenishment expected.

Record the exact SKU, warehouse and observation time. A stale feed is an observation, not a fresh promise. For an existing order, obtain the acknowledgment and the expected handoff separately from generic listing availability.

**Reservation reverses by seller.** A seller with steady sales history and spare cash can ask for a deposit-backed reservation while the supplier keeps shipping direct. A new seller with neither should not tie up cash that way: the cash and unsold-stock cost can outweigh the stockout risk. Before treating a reservation as secured supply, get its terms:

- quantity and expiration;
- release rules and cancellation;
- storage;
- unused-stock disposition.

A deposit alone does not specify ownership or insolvency protection.

If the supplier accepts a business card, spendable Whop proceeds can fund a repeat order or agreed reservation before a bank payout arrives. Compare the charge currency, supplier surcharge and any advance fee with waiting or negotiating later payment. Preserve funding for orders already sold and likely remedies. Earlier payment buys no stock rights by itself: obtain quantity, release terms and dispatch commitment. Wire-only suppliers still need the bank timeline.

**When stock runs short, change the promise before the purchase.** Put the real availability date beside the buy button. Stop paying for traffic to items that are weeks from restock, and point browsers to a genuinely available alternative. Offer an alternative to existing buyers only as a disclosed choice; never quietly swap an item that was already ordered. Supply facts go to the storefront, support and ad owners. This analysis does not authorize changing those systems.

**Build the delivery statement from its legs:**

1. time before the order is accepted;
2. preparation and cutoff;
3. carrier acceptance;
4. route transit;
5. customs where applicable;
6. last mile.

Preserve business-day versus calendar-day distinctions. A carrier estimate often begins at acceptance, not checkout. Use an evidenced range for the selected route rather than adding averages from unrelated shipments. A ship-by date shown on a storefront should come from this evidence.

Compare waiting, a reservation with documented rights, alternate stock and a revised availability promise. Include the cash tied up and unsold-stock exposure.

## Deliverable and checks

- an availability and dispatch evidence sheet with stale or unknown flags;
- proposed promise wording for the storefront owner;
- a reservation decision, if requested.

Hand supply constraints to `ad-campaign-scaling`; this skill does not change ads itself.
