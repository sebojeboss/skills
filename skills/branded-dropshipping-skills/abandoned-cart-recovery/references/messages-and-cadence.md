# Write the recovery sequence

## When to read

Drafting, revising, choosing timing, segmentation or offers. A narrow copy request can finish here with supplied product and policy facts.

## Order the messages by what the shopper probably still needs

Start with what the shopper did and what is still unresolved. Adding to cart is weaker intent than starting checkout, but neither tells you the motive. So begin with the simplest explanation: they got distracted.

The first message is a reminder. Show the item they chose, give a direct way back to the cart, and add one line that lowers the risk: the actual returns terms, delivery time, or what the price works out to per use. Hold the discount. Many shoppers come back without one, and an early coupon pays them to do what they were going to do anyway.

If they haven't returned, earn trust next: real reviews of this product, what makes it different, any genuine third-party recognition. Then answer the doubt specific to the category. For frozen food, that's whether it arrives cold. For a bag organizer, whether it fits the bag. For clothing, sizing and exchanges. Explain the actual process or policy; a coupon doesn't answer a freshness question.

Price comes last, and only where it's justified: never-buyers who are eligible for the store's first-order offer, or a shipping charge that surprised them at checkout. There's one exception. If the site already promised a first-order discount, put it in the first message, because holding back an offer the shopper has already seen feels like a bait. Returning customers get help, not the acquisition discount.

A last message can ask what stopped them, from an inbox someone reads. Use replies to choose the next doubt to answer.

Another order can fit. A product whose main hesitation is known in advance, such as fit for a bag organizer, can lead with that doubt. A store whose campaigns already carry reviews and brand story may skip the trust message. Change the order when you know the hesitation; keep it when you're guessing.

Segment only where the message changes: never-bought versus returning customer, a known concern, product category, offer eligibility. Don't add branches just because data exists. Don't invent reservations, scarcity, guarantees or deadlines; if there's no real deadline, there's no "last chance". Text-style email can reduce visual distraction. It is not necessarily plain text, or a personal message actually typed by the named sender, so don't invent personal attention.

## How long, and how soon

Length depends on what else the shopper receives. A store sending campaigns three or four times a week can stop at about four recovery emails, because the campaigns keep the contact going. A store with little other contact may justify a longer tail. But an order credited to the eighth email isn't proof the eighth email caused it; that shopper may have come back anyway. Test the tail by randomly suppressing it for some eligible shoppers rather than by reading attributed revenue.

Timing has the same trap. Shoppers who left thirty minutes ago are among the likeliest to come back on their own, so an early first send can look strong in the flow report simply because it gets credit for purchases already on their way. A later send reaches only shoppers who didn't come back by themselves, so its attributed rate looks worse even when it adds more orders. Compare delays against shoppers who received nothing, assigned when they abandoned; [incremental contribution](incremental-contribution.md) works through an illustrative contrast. There is no universal best first delay or message count. Keep a proven cadence unless the problem at hand calls for a change.

## Worked drafts

Illustrative product: a washable travel organizer with a verified dimensions page and a 30-day returns policy whose cost to the shopper is stated on the policy page. These are drafts, not observed results. Every bracketed or policy claim must match the store's actual terms.

**Reminder.** Subject: "Your travel organizer is still in your cart". Preheader: "[Verified returns line, e.g. '30-day returns']". Write "free returns" only if the policy says the shopper pays nothing for return shipping or restocking. Body: "You left the [color] organizer in your cart. It measures [verified dimensions]. Not sure it fits your bag? The size guide puts the measurements side by side." CTA: "Back to my cart". Use a tested dynamic link, and don't imply the item is reserved.

**Doubt.** Subject: "Will it fit your bag?" Body: "Compare your bag's usable interior dimensions and opening with the organizer's verified dimensions in the size guide. Reply with your bag's measurements and we'll help check the fit." CTA: "See the size guide". Drop the reply line if no one monitors the inbox.

**Ask.** Subject: "Quick question about your cart". Body: "You looked at the travel organizer but didn't order. Was it the size, the price, delivery time, or something else? A one-word reply helps." Send it from a named person only if that person or their team actually reads the replies.

**Offer, only if authorized.** "Your [actual offer] applies to [eligible items] until [real deadline and timezone]." Put the restrictions beside the button. A returning buyer can get the doubt message without this one.

## Check the draft

Deliver the copy with its segment, delay rationale, supported facts, exits and response owner. Check mobile subject/preheader clipping and image-blocked output. Use the optional [message brief](message-brief.md) to align several drafts. Keep cadence alternatives and the evidence that would change the choice; compare contribution separately from attributed revenue.
