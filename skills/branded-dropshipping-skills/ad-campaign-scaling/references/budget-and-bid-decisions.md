# Budget and bid decisions

## When to read

Load for budget changes, cost/bid/ROAS controls, intraday scaling, sale periods, duplication proposals or a failed scaling attempt. The aim is an explicit allocation choice, not a universal percentage rule.

## How to do the work

There are four moves: hold, raise the existing allocation, move spend between economically distinct units, or reduce/pause. For the one you propose, say what it should gain, the evidence for it, the most it can lose before you review it, and what it gives up. Judge the account total, not one campaign. A newly busy campaign can simply be taking delivery from another.

Work out the room you actually have first. Allowable cost per purchase is what one acquired order can spend on advertising and still meet the merchant's target, given contribution before ads and the payback horizon the cash can carry (see [acquisition contribution](acquisition-contribution-and-funding.md)). A store that has to recover ad spend on the first order has less room than one that can wait for repeat orders and has the cash to wait.

For a control change, keep the auction bid separate from the reported acquisition cost. Meta's [bid-strategy documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/ads-commerce/marketing-api/bidding/overview/bid-strategy) says adherence to a cost cap is not guaranteed and a capped campaign may not spend its full budget. A bid cap limits the auction bid, which is not the cost you'll see in reporting. A minimum-ROAS benchmark set too high can underdeliver. Lowest-cost bidding gives no cost control, and costs rise as cheaper opportunities run out or the budget grows. Which strategies, fields and combinations an account can use, and how edits affect learning, change over time. Check them in the current account and documentation at the moment of the change rather than relying on an old UI label or a remembered rule.

## Worked decisions

All numbers below are illustrative.

**Judge the increment, not the campaign.** A product spending about $80 a day brings in roughly six store orders a day. You raise it to $120, and the next two days also bring about six each. That doesn't prove the extra $40 did nothing. Two days is a small sample, demand moves day to day, and some of those clicks may convert later. But so far the increment hasn't shown it pays for itself, even though the campaign still looks profitable on average. Decide what finding out is worth: hold the higher budget for a set number of days, or until a set amount of extra spend you can afford to lose, then compare store orders and contribution per day at each level, allowing for conversion lag. If the higher level still isn't adding orders worth more than they cost, go back to the budget that worked and keep the product; the evidence is against the larger budget, not the product. If the next increment has to pay for itself now, step back sooner. Count store orders as well as attributed purchases, and compare account totals so a campaign isn't credited with spend it took from another.

**Raise a winner in place; don't move it.** Experienced buyers rarely turn off a working ad to rebuild it inside a new "scaling campaign". The rebuild throws away the delivery history that money already bought. Raise the existing budget and watch the account total. The exception is a break, not a disappointment. Normally cost per purchase drifts up gradually as spend rises. If a raise is instead followed by results collapsing toward zero, a fresh duplicate at the higher budget, with the original paused, is a reasonable workaround. It gives up the known delivery mix and may not restore performance, so treat it as temporary and keep the original available to switch back.

**Underdelivery can be the control doing its job.** Before relaxing a cost cap, bid cap or ROAS goal that "won't spend", find out whether it was tightened on purpose. A merchant who raised the ROAS goal ahead of a stockout and forgot to lower it has a different problem from one whose allowance is simply too low for this product. Then rule out the delivery faults that look the same: an ad or account restriction, a broken or changed conversion event, an audience, placement or schedule that's too narrow, or the wrong optimization goal. If those check out and the allowance is right, the likely explanation is economic: at that price there aren't enough auctions you can afford. Either accept the lower volume as the cost of protecting margin, or change the creative, offer, page or product so more auctions become affordable. Don't buy volume you can't pay for. If the allowance was set too tight, recompute it from actual contribution, then loosen in one bounded step. Hold the daily budget at what you could afford to lose over a few bad days while you watch, because a target doesn't cap losses. A bid cap set a little above allowable cost per purchase is one way to constrain cost. It is not the default, and it can fail to spend or misbehave against a custom conversion event, so compare it with the current setup rather than switching the whole account. When moving campaigns to new settings, start with a secondary spender, not the top one.

**Plan a promotion's entry and exit.** First recompute allowable cost per purchase at the discounted price and the sale's basket mix. A discount can shrink your room even while conversion rises. Then decide which error is worse for this business: missing sale volume or paying too much per customer. Leaving controls at their normal targets and accepting less volume is a legitimate choice. Some operators instead raise a bid limit temporarily, on the theory that the system's conversion predictions catch up slowly to a real sale-day jump. That behavior isn't documented, so treat it as a bounded experiment: use a smaller budget than instinct says as the loss envelope, record edit times, and lower the bid toward the real allowance as purchases come in. Schedule the reversal before the sale starts. The day after a sale, conversion falls back, and a campaign still on sale settings can overspend badly.

**Surfing a good morning.** "Surf scaling" means pushing budget up during a strong day and resetting it in the evening. Morning purchases partly reflect yesterday's clicks, and the next hour's spend buys different auctions. If you try it, log edit times and the account timezone, compare against campaigns you didn't touch, and set the reset time and loss limit before you start.

No fixed safe-increase percentage, mandatory wait period or surfing trigger is established here. Raising an existing budget, adding concepts and broadening the audience all get called scaling, but they change different things, so name the actual change.

## Deliverable and checks

Give the option chosen and the ones rejected, with the exact proposed values, the economic bound, what it sacrifices, the review time and the rollback condition. Say which budget owns delivery (campaign or ad set), whether the amount is daily or lifetime, and that a daily budget is not an exact daily-spend ceiling. Check that the field, strategy and operation are available in the current account before calling the proposal executable, then follow [execution and reconciliation](execute-and-reconcile-changes.md).
