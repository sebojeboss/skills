# Discover through ad libraries

## When to read

Use the native library to verify a Foreplay lead, investigate missing coverage or work when live Foreplay access is unavailable. For the main search workflow, read [Foreplay discovery to shortlist](foreplay-discovery-to-shortlist.md). Start from a public product keyword, advertiser, ad or target market; follow competitor → ad inventory → actual product destination → supplier match and catalog neighbors.

## Hunt competitors before products

Open the native [Meta Ad Library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library/) in an available browser. Set the country, or a few markets you could actually ship to, and the ad category, keep only active ads, and search a broad category word. Use whatever date, status, platform and media controls the page currently shows, and record what you selected; labels and filters change, so don't rely on an old walkthrough's selectors.

Open advertisers, not just ads. The first question is whether this account is an operator testing products the way you would. An established brand whose trust, repeat customers and margins you can't borrow goes on the watchlist, not into the candidate list. A small advertiser selling exactly what your audience wants beats a large account selling everything. Keep advertisers you'd learn from even when none of their products qualifies; note the market and why they're worth rechecking.

## Read the inventory for what to inspect next

Active listings show what an advertiser has published, not what it spends or sells. Read them to decide which advertisers and products deserve a closer look:

- **Volume.** A store with dozens of active ads across many products is publishing a lot of variations. That makes it worth studying which products and bodies it keeps. Volume is a reason to look closer, not a gate, and an interesting small account deserves the same inspection.
- **Start dates.** Operators who test many products often cut a loser quickly. If this advertiser does prune (revisit it, or compare an older capture, and look for short-lived ads that have disappeared), an ad still active days after launch is worth inspecting early. If it doesn't prune, the same ad tells you little. An ad running for months shows a message that has stayed published. You're late, copies likely exist, and it may simply have been forgotten. Choose which you're hunting for, early entry or persistence; one ad can't give you both.
- **Duplicates and distinct bodies.** Look at what varies. The same creative republished in newer ads, the same product returning with new footage, or one opening tried with several different bodies all show where the advertiser keeps working. Relaunches, placement tests and housekeeping produce the same picture, so treat it as a reason to inspect that product, not as evidence of spend. Group repeats by product and creative family, keep each ad ID, and don't count several crops of one video as several confirmations.
- **Dates.** The displayed start date, your observation date and a tool's "last seen" are different facts. Record them separately. Revisiting the same advertiser a few days later, with the same filters, is how you learn whether it prunes.

## Follow the ad to the store

Open the destination and identify what's actually sold: product, variant, price ladder, bundles, and what shipping costs once you reach checkout. A destination can be a collection or an advertorial rather than the product, so follow the route far enough to find the item. Routine reversible cart inspection implied by the task needs no extra confirmation; purchases and sensitive submissions do. Notice careless-store tells, such as a surprise shipping fee at checkout or a throwaway store name. They can explain a competitor's weak result and show you where you could do better.

Then take the main product image to a public reverse-image search and supplier catalogs. You're looking for the supplier listing, its similar items, and how hard it was to find. If you can't find it, that's a gap in your search, not proof the product is scarce: the match may be poor, the listing renamed, the item different or out of stock. Read [exact SKU and supply](exact-sku-and-supply-feasibility.md) for what to ask a sourcing contact before spending on traffic.

## One business, several Pages

The library shows Pages, not ad accounts. One business often advertises from several: a store Page, regional Pages, a founder's personal Page, creators running ads for it. Before concluding that a competitor "only runs a few ads", check which domains its ads send traffic to and look for other Pages pointing there. Shared domains and similar names are clues, not proof that two Pages share an account.

## Paid aggregators

A spy tool can find leads quickly. Return to the native ad and the store wherever possible, and label any spend or revenue figure with its provider, period and basis; they're model outputs. Some free tools publish lookups as public, indexed pages about the searched business. Check that before typing a competitor's domain into one. Duplicates can reflect testing or housekeeping as easily as expansion, and a missing result means only "not found in this capture's scope".

## Worked decisions

Illustrative supplied facts: a category search in two markets turns up a large account running ads for dozens of unrelated accessories, and a smaller account with eight active ads, three of them for a compact wall-mounted tool rack. Two of the rack ads started this week. One started five weeks ago and was republished with a new body two days ago. A capture from last week shows the account had several other products that are now gone. The user sells to a workshop audience. Inspect the smaller account first, because fit matters more than size. The vanished ads suggest this advertiser prunes, and the rack is the product it keeps republishing with new bodies, which makes the rack the lead to check against an exact supplier listing. That's a reason to investigate, not a spend or sales estimate. The store also sells a freestanding version. Keep the two as separate variants, because mounting changes the buyer and the demonstration.

## Deliverable and checks

Keep the query, filters, advertiser/ad links, capture date, creative family and exact destination with each candidate decision. Stop when new families repeat the same hypothesis or a supply or buyer question becomes decisive. If blocked, retain completed observations and the missing scope; never bypass access controls. Repeated capture and full teardowns belong to competitor research.

An official ads API is optional, and only after its current access and commercial-ad coverage are verified. Don't promise browser/API parity or invent a scraping endpoint.
