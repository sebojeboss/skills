# From concept to a complete ad

## When to read

Read for a complete ad, a change of angle, or moving a working ad into another format. A crop or caption correction stays a crop or caption correction; don't reopen an approved argument just to offer alternatives.

## How to do the work

Pick the angle before the execution. The angle is the buying reason; the format is the vehicle (creator demo, founder ad, longer sales video, static); the execution is everything you can see. "A dramatic product spin" is an execution. It doesn't tell the viewer why the item matters. Write the takeaway in one plain sentence, then the reason to believe it, the visual event that makes it understandable, and what the viewer must know before the call to action makes sense. For writing the body itself (mechanism, outcome, proof beats and the ask), read [the selling argument](selling-argument.md).

Let the assets you actually have shape the concept early. A side view nobody shot, or a mechanism nobody can film, can make a simple-sounding idea expensive. Genuine footage of the thing working often beats a more polished reconstruction of it. Inventory what each asset can honestly show (identity and scale, the buyer's situation, the operation, the result, the offer) before committing to a concept the material can't carry.

For a static, arrange a recognizable product, one intelligible claim, the evidence needed to interpret it, and the next action. Decide the reading order at actual phone size: what is noticed first, what is understood second, and what is read only by someone already interested. A single image can't show motion, so a hinge that locks may need a two- or three-panel sequence or a clear before and after. For video, spread the opening, explanation or demonstration, offer and ask across the material you have. These jobs can overlap; they are not equal-length shots, and the shot count follows the tool's real constraints and the edit, not a template.

When a message has already sold in one format, moving it to another is usually cheaper than inventing a new argument: a long video cut to a short one, a script handed to a creator, a winning line turned into a static headline. Carry over what did the selling: the buying reason, the line that explains why it works, the proof that answered the main doubt. Expect the new vehicle to need its own opening and often its own way of demonstrating. A simple cause-and-fix fits in fifteen seconds; a long root-cause story usually doesn't. Check what the new format can actually carry before cutting the proof to fit. If a static loses the demonstration that made the video believable, solve that proof problem before calling it ready. The move is a new test, not an inherited win.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: an adjustable desk lamp, with a verified arm reach and owned footage of the joint being repositioned. Three directions come up. A static close-up of the joint with the measured reach makes the adjustment legible at a glance but can't show how easily it moves. A short video of someone swinging the arm from keyboard to sketchbook shows the use but needs a clean, uninterrupted take. A lamp floating against clouds makes a beautiful hero image and says nothing about adjustment. Keep the floating lamp only if atmosphere is its job and the rest of the ad supplies the reason; otherwise it spends the opening on the wrong promise. None of the three gets an eye-health benefit; nothing supplied supports one.

If someone asks for a new crop of an approved ad, the argument isn't open. If they ask why an ad is hard to follow, check whether a key premise is missing before polishing transitions: viewers can't care about an outcome when they never learned what was at stake.

## Deliverable and checks

Return copy plus layout or shot arrangement and the call to action together, not a mood board. Name assumptions and missing evidence in the working brief. Say whether you changed the concept (buying reason), the format (vehicle) or only the execution, so later results can be read against the right change.
