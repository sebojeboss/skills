# Choose an offer worth taking

Read when the add-on, the bundle contents or what follows a declined offer is still open. If the user has already chosen the kit or upsell, resolve its missing details rather than reopening the choice by habit.

## Start from what the front-end product leaves unfinished

A good add-on does one of two jobs for the person who just chose the main product. It makes the result they bought arrive better, faster or easier: the connector that fits the hose, a second filter so the first one is never empty mid-week. Or it solves the problem the main product creates once it works: somewhere to store the tool set, a way to keep a new routine going once the first result arrives. If you can't say which job an item does for this buyer, the relevance is yours, not theirs. Frequently-bought-together data, a high margin or a supplier's spare SKU can make an item easy to offer without making it worth accepting.

Choose the add-on when you choose the product. An offer bolted on after launch tends to be whatever the catalog had. One chosen alongside the main product can shape the product page, the bundle tiers and the first-order economics. If no honest add-on exists yet, launch without one: an empty offer slot is better than an irrelevant one. Note what evidence would justify adding one later.

Build a **starter kit** around getting first use right. Build a **retention bundle** around a returning customer's next need. A returning customer's response doesn't predict a cold buyer's, so keep the two results apart.

## Frequently bought together is a lead, not a verdict

Pull two separate views of the orders: items bought in the same order, and first purchase followed by the next paid purchase, with the time between them. They answer different questions.

For same-order pairs, compare how often the pair appears with how often the complement sells at all. A popular accessory turns up beside everything without being especially relevant to this product. Count retained purchases only. Canceled lines, refunds and repeated rows can produce a convincing-looking bundle nobody actually kept.

For sequences, ask what changed between the two purchases. A buyer who returns for the same insole in a firmer density may have needed to prove fit first; look for that explanation in their feedback or use history. If fit was the gate, bundling both densities into the first order asks for the second commitment before that proof and raises the entry price. A storage pouch bought weeks later may simply have been unknown at the time, making it a candidate to offer earlier. Use the sequence to choose which explanation to investigate, not to assume that delaying caused the second sale.

Every serious option carries a sacrifice. A complete kit saves shopping effort but raises the entry price. A quantity pack can improve shipping cost per unit but postpones the next order. Keep the benefit and the sacrifice together when you compare options.

## When a relevant offer loses, find the question the buyer couldn't answer

Relevance doesn't guarantee acceptance. These failure patterns look alike in a take-rate report and each needs a different repair:

- **Fit is unresolved.** A protective case offered beside a device accessory loses if the buyer has to leave the cart to check which model it fits. Some never come back. Put the model or size selector inside the offer; here more choice removes uncertainty.
- **The only new choice is to spend less.** A refill the buyer already understands doesn't need a size menu. Add one to a post-purchase offer and acceptance may hardly move while buyers drift to the smallest size, so revenue per offer view falls. Here less choice is the repair.
- **Too much, too soon.** Two extra units can lose to one when the buyer hasn't used the first. A three-pack can beat a single when the product runs out fast. Check how quickly it is consumed before choosing the quantity; neither direction is a rule.
- **A commitment hidden in the default.** A pre-selected subscription add-on on the product page can raise AOV among buyers while fewer people buy at all, especially on mobile, where it takes over the screen. Judge it on revenue or contribution per visitor, not order value.
- **It was hard to find.** If a quiz or a new layout lifts revenue, check whether it simply exposed bundles the navigation was hiding. A clearer bundle display may do the same job with one fewer step.
- **It works too well.** A one-click accessory can sell through its stock and take the offer down with it, or the main product's delivery promise. Check component stock before rollout.

## A downsell needs a new reason to say yes

A familiar downsell tactic is the same product at a steep discount, sometimes half price. Test a price-only downsell when price was the objection and the discounted order still meets the contribution target. It also reveals that the earlier price was negotiable, and repeat customers can learn to say no. If the objection was too much product, offer one unit or the smaller kit. If it was the wrong item, offer the other complement. If there was no need, end the sequence and protect the relationship. Call it a "one-time offer" only if it won't be offered again.

Compare downsells after the same rejection, with the same eligibility, before giving the discount the credit. Calculate take rate among buyers actually shown an eligible offer, then look at profit per visitor across the whole route. Higher AOV among the remaining buyers can hide fewer purchases or worse economics.

## Choose and state the reversal condition

Illustrative garden kit: "Offer the compatible connector beside the hose because it completes installation. Ask connector size here; don't default to the most popular size. Offer a second hose only where the customer needs another watering area, not because it doubles the basket." Reverse the recommendation if the hose already includes the connector or if fit can't be resolved reliably.
