# Coordinate speech, image and performance

## When to read

Use this to design a spoken/visual opening, assess conflicting messages or give a creator/editor concrete direction. Text review alone can support copy decisions, but it cannot settle an unheard performance or unseen action.

## How to do the work

Inspect what each channel contributes at the consequential moments. Speech may name the task, the overlay may identify the intended viewer, and the image may demonstrate the problem. They need a coherent relationship, not necessarily identical information. Repetition can confirm a promise or aid comprehension; eliminating every repeated word can remove useful reinforcement.

For a complex opening, make a short beat map:

| Beat | Spoken information | Visible text/action | What becomes understandable | Remaining question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First frame | What is actually said | What can actually be seen | Premise now available | Question the next beat can develop |
| First continuation | New information | Consequential action or proof | Relationship made clearer | Next meaningful uncertainty |

Use actual observations before interpretation. A thumbnail is not a viewed sequence. An enlarged frame may reveal a small label that normal playback does not make readable. Recognizing a container is not the same as understanding which latch matters. A cultural reference may delight the intended audience while confusing another; decide whom the opening is for rather than explaining everything indiscriminately.

Choose edits by communication job. A crop can isolate the relevant control; a return to a wide view can restore where that control sits. A completed action may need a hold before the next instruction. Speeding up setup can be useful while speeding through the crucial release makes the demonstration harder to follow. Decorative effects are optional: remove a vignette if it obscures the very edge being discussed. A freeze may remove a blinking cursor that competes with the finished state. Preserve the useful original when a more elaborate version loses orientation.

**A-roll** is the main performance; **B-roll** is supporting footage. Use B-roll to show what speech cannot efficiently establish, make a process concrete or supply a useful comic contrast. It need not cover every sentence. Staying with the speaker can preserve a reaction, connection or moment of encouragement. A literal screen recording and a comic enactment serve different purposes; choose according to the task.

For delivery, write toward one listener who can use or enjoy the material. Concrete recognizable wording often gives a speaker more to mean than abstract instructions to “sound authentic.” Read aloud if that helps identify mouthfuls, weak emphasis or logical gaps. Preserve the speaker's own humor and rhythm. A teleprompter is compatible with natural performance; the problem is whether the delivery fits the intended address, not whether preparation occurred.

Do not equate naturalness with zero editing or energy with constant speed. A breath may interrupt a dense list, support a joke, or allow the visual to register. Inspect its role before removing it. Recommendations about warmth, cadence, emphasis and intelligibility must be tied to actual audio inspection; a transcript can suggest performance options but cannot confirm them.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative brief: show how a storage container opens with one latch. The first frame shows the closed container, the overlay identifies the task, and speech invites the demonstration.

An initial tight crop emphasizes the latch but hides the finger's release. Accelerating it makes the cause-and-effect relationship still less clear. Restore a wider view for the release, then move closer if the detail remains useful. Hold the open state long enough to identify what changed. The sacrifice is some visual intensity; the gain is intelligibility.

Now compare two versions of a screen tutorial. The first displays the full interface while naming one small button. The second isolates the button but removes its surrounding location. A useful repair can first establish the full view, then isolate the control, then show the result. Neither “always zoom” nor “never show a complex screen” solves both problems.

Finally, a speaker's quiet amused pause follows a visible mishap. Removing it makes the clip faster but removes the reaction that gives the moment its personality. Keep it if that reaction serves the invitation. If the same silence is merely a recording hesitation before an unrelated instruction, cutting it may help. The decision requires the sequence, not a pause-duration threshold.

## Deliverable and checks

Provide a paired opening, concise creator direction or timecoded edit notes appropriate to the task. State the intended visual action precisely enough to execute: what must become visible, what should remain in frame, and where the completed state matters. Separate a suggested performance from an observed one. Full filming, generation, compositing and export belong to production work.
