# Critique and revise the actual problem

## When to read

Use this when critique, shortening, repair or restoration is requested, especially with fixed footage or creator pushback. Do not turn ordinary drafting into a compulsory critique loop.

## How to do the work

Preserve settled choices: a selected first line, recorded footage, deliberate joke, runtime or product fact. If delivery matters, inspect that mode or narrow the judgment. Keep what works.

When the failure is unclear, check delay, confusion, irrelevance, then disinterest. More intrigue cannot repair an unclear subject or supply a reason to care. Use this order as a diagnostic aid; skip it when the problem is obvious or the request is narrower.

| Failure | What to inspect | Possible repair |
|---|---|---|
| **Delay**: it takes too long to learn what this is about | Where the subject first becomes clear in speech, text or image | Move the subject forward, or let the first frame carry it while the line catches up |
| **Confusion**: the subject is there, but the line can be read two ways | Which word carries the relationship; what a stranger could mistake it for | Restore the connecting clause. Shorter isn't clearer if it drops the link |
| **Irrelevance**: understood, but the viewer has no reason to care | Whose situation the opening names | Name the viewer's moment or stake. First person is fine when the speaker's stake is real and relevant |
| **Disinterest**: clear and relevant, but nothing is left to find out | What question remains after the first line; whether the outcome seems ordinary because a rule, relationship or constraint is missing | Leave a real question (the method, the outcome, the choice), and supply any missing premise earlier |

Past the opening, locate the first consequential break in understanding or fulfillment:

| Observed issue | What to inspect | Possible repair |
|---|---|---|
| Opening and next beat concern different things | What each sentence/image makes the viewer expect | Replace the continuation or deliberately change the invitation |
| The important event gets little space | Backstory versus promised moment | Reduce irrelevant setup and expand the actual event |
| Proof seems absent | Entire sequence, overlays, source status and viewing conditions | Correct the review if already present; then examine relevance/placement |
| Several facts feel disconnected | Causal or contrast relationship between them | State the real relationship instead of stacking facts |
| Edit feels busy but difficult to follow | Orientation, completed actions and competing information | Restore a simpler view or hold; remove only the distraction |
| Copy feels unlike the speaker | Actual speech, humor and intended address | Preserve concrete personal phrasing rather than generic polish |

These are discriminators, not a required list of faults. “No change needed” is valid. A missing premise and a deliberately unresolved outcome are different: the first prevents interest from forming, while the second can sustain interest once the situation is understood.

When the creator corrects a factual reading, update the critique. Proof can be present yet fail to register for one reviewer. Check whether the reviewer saw the whole passage, whether the proof's status is clear and whether the connection is expressed. Do not infer a universal audience failure from an interrupted viewing. “Add proof” can be the wrong repair when the actual problem is relevance, sequencing or a mistaken reviewer impression.

Choose the smallest coherent intervention. With fixed footage, changing an overlay that asks the wrong question may be enough. When the script promises two different experiences, separate pieces may serve each better than a forced connection. When a decorative treatment obscures the target, restoring the earlier version is a real creative decision. Retain the rejected variant and reason when iteration warrants a record; do not create version bureaucracy for a one-word correction.

When choosing among drafted or generated options, judge parts. Often the first half of one line and the second half of another are the material worth keeping. A shorter rewrite can lose the plain wording that made the longer one easy, and a line that ticks every box on paper can be the one a speaker stumbles over. Say why an option won. "I like this one" tells the next writer nothing; "it names the exact moment the cable drops" does.

Read aloud when it helps diagnose flow. Notice incomplete logic, cumbersome phrases and emphasis that no longer fits the meaning. Stop when the refinement has served the task. Neither a fixed pass cap nor endless polish is a quality standard.

## Worked decisions

**A question the footage cannot answer.** Illustrative: a pouch video has an approved spoken opening: “One pouch, two ways to carry it.” The recorded body shows shoulder and waist configurations. An added overlay asks, “Which one holds more?” A reviewer proposes new capacity-test footage, but that would expand the assignment and investigate a difference the two carrying positions do not establish.

Keep the selected line and footage. Change the overlay to “Shoulder or waist?” and let each configuration become visible before the next transition. The repair is to the competing invitation, not the product demonstration. If the user actually wants a capacity comparison between different pouches, that is a separate brief requiring different evidence. Do not quietly transform a carry-options edit into that job.

**A reviewer corrects the diagnosis.** Illustrative: a screen tutorial opens with “Find the latest draft without renaming your files.” After the presenter selects a column heading, the filenames move to different rows. Reviewing only cropped stills, an editor concludes that files were deleted and recommends a recovery warning.

The presenter asks the editor to inspect the continuous recording. It shows the same filenames before and after the action, with the date column sorted in the opposite direction. The editor revises the feedback: “I confused reordering with deletion. Those files are still present. The recovery warning would describe an action this recording does not show.” Withdraw that warning before making further suggestions.

Now assess the actual viewing conditions. If the column heading and sort indicator are readable in the intended export, leave the sequence alone; the reviewer's incomplete inspection is not a defect in the video. If the delivery crop removes those controls, restore enough of the interface to identify the sort action. That repair sacrifices some filename magnification to preserve cause and effect. Adding reassurance about backups would not explain why the rows moved.

Keep observed defects, proposed repairs and completed edits distinct. Explain a consequential change when useful; do not add an alternative merely to demonstrate criticism.
