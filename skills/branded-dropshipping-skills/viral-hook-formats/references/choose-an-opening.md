# Choose an opening

## When to read

Use this when choosing a premise, generating different hooks or diagnosing why a familiar sentence template feels empty. A hook is the opening experience across words, image and action, not necessarily one spoken sentence.

## How to do the work

Recover the particular experience being offered. “Learn about storage” is broad; “see how this rack fits behind a door” names something the footage can show. A viewer may also want to test a guess, recognize an annoyance, enjoy a person's company or discover whether an attempt succeeds. Do not convert every subject into a pain-resolution lesson.

Write a plain description of what is interesting in the available material before polishing the first line. Identify what the intended **cold viewer**—someone without the creator's earlier posts—can already know and what the opening must supply. A name, object or cultural reference can orient the right audience; it can also leave the central relationship unexplained. Specificity earns its place when it makes the situation imaginable, comparable or useful.

Treat the opening as an offer. The viewer pays in seconds, so the first moment has to say what they get, why they should believe this speaker can deliver it, and roughly what it'll cost them. Each lever works differently. The outcome can be made more wanted or more unusual. Belief can come from plainer wording, a result already visible on screen, the setting, or the speaker showing they understand the viewer's situation by describing it more exactly than the viewer would. A bounded promise ("three fixes in thirty seconds") tells them the value per second before they commit. A low-effort framing ("the lazy way to…") makes the outcome feel within reach. Pull one or two levers hard. An opening that tries to tick every box (curiosity, pain, promised solution, specificity, credibility, time frame) gets long enough to lose the simplicity that made it work.

Choose an entry that the material can fulfill:

| Entry | Reason to continue | Body obligation | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Result first | Find out how the shown result happened | Demonstrate the method or consequential steps | Showing a result and then discussing something else |
| Real attempt or constraint | Discover whether the attempt succeeds | Actual effort, setbacks and outcome | Fake deadline, impossible-to-fail challenge or success already announced |
| Concrete problem history | Understand why this route differs from earlier attempts | Show the relevant difference | Invented time, cost or personal frustration |
| Comparison | Resolve a meaningful choice | Comparable conditions and a useful distinction | Different products or circumstances presented as an isolated test |
| Recognition or POV | Recognize a moment, social situation or feeling | The interaction or experience named by the overlay | “POV” attached to footage that does not depict the situation |
| Participation | Make a choice or test recognition | An intelligible task and response | A quiz requiring knowledge the intended viewer cannot plausibly have |
| Relevant disagreement | Examine a surprising alternative | Explain the actual disagreement | A dramatic “wrong” with no substantive difference |
| Immediate useful item | Get a usable answer without delay | Begin the first item and fulfill the bounded promise | Repeating why the information matters instead of giving it |
| Immediate offer | Assess an already relevant deal | Real terms, eligibility and product fit | Treating a discovery audience as if already ready to buy |

These are overlapping choices, not a sorting test. A result preview can create curiosity about a method without telling a transformation story. A founder introduction can establish relevant stakes while a generic name-and-job introduction merely consumes time.

Draft different invitations before drafting synonyms. Changing “amazing” to “incredible” does not explore a new format. Changing “will it fit?” to “here is how it folds” changes what the body must do. Once the invitation is chosen, make the first line say only what is needed to establish it. Keep the interesting substance; compression that removes the premise is a loss.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: supplied footage shows a laundry rack folded behind a door, unfolded across a narrow room, and loaded with clothes. Its rated capacity is unknown.

- **Method preview:** “This is where the drying rack goes when the laundry's done.” Show the folded rack, then the hinge movement. The result is visible; the remaining question is how it gets there.
- **Attempt:** “Can this wash fit without blocking the doorway?” Show the real load and available space. The body must show the actual outcome, including failure if that is what happened. Do not attach the first version's success overlay.
- **Comparison:** “Look at what happens to the walkway when these two racks open.” Show both in the same room under relevant conditions. This promises a space comparison, not stronger construction or faster drying.
- **Recognition:** “The laundry is drying. The chair has lost its home again.” The scene supplies an ordinary inconvenience. Continue into the actual rearrangement rather than a general productivity speech.

The attempt is unavailable if the supplied footage never records the attempt. The comparison is unavailable without a defensible second setup. The preview may therefore be the most useful option even if the challenge sounds more dramatic. A truthful, filmable opening is more usable than a clever line that creates missing evidence.
