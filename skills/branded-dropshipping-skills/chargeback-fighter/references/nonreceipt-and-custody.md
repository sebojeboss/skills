# Nonreceipt and custody

## When to read

Use for missing, delayed, held, rerouted, forwarded or collected goods. Proof of delivery, often called POD, proves a particular carrier event—not every fact about the purchase.

## How to do the work

Recover the delivery promise visible at purchase, dispatch record, tracking history, destination, delivery event and recipient linkage. Preserve timezones and the order-to-parcel join. Explain a workplace, gift recipient or forwarding address using records rather than assuming the name mismatch proves fraud. Distinguish delivery to the requested intermediary from delivery to the final user, and identify which leg the merchant promised.

For collection, find the actual pickup record or identity verification event. “The carrier normally checks ID” is weaker than a record that it did so. For rerouting, separate buyer request, merchant permission, carrier acceptance and actual destination. A carrier claim may address a carrier failure without reversing chargeback liability.

If tracking is in transit, report that state. Check whether waiting for a new event still leaves a valid response opportunity under the actual cutoff and provider editing rules. Never wait beyond the deadline hoping delivery will fix the case. A replacement shipment adds inventory exposure; it does not automatically resolve the first dispute. Download the necessary evidence into an accepted format; a tracking URL may not be reviewed by an issuer.

## Worked decisions

Illustrative: a parcel addressed to an office is signed for by reception. A purchase email identifies that office as the requested destination. Use that connection, the scan and the delivery time; do not claim personal receipt by the cardholder. If the buyer instead says the box was empty, the same scan answers only custody; route to [not-as-described](not-as-described-and-returns.md) as well.

When the claim is about contents, look for a fact that helps distinguish the competing accounts: packed weight against the item's known weight, a packing photo tied to the order number, or a mismatch in the parcel records. Illustrative: a buyer reports an empty box, but the carrier recorded a collection weight close to the packed item's weight, and the buyer's photo shows a label whose tracking number belongs to a different parcel. Check the carrier's weight record and the photo's parcel identity. Collection weight bears on what was handed to the carrier; it doesn't establish what arrived. The label mismatch may mean the wrong photo was supplied. Explain each fact's bearing on the allegation and the unresolved gap instead of burying it in generic delivery records or calling it proof of fraud.

A second illustration: a parcel arrives after the initial submission locked. Preserve the new delivery record, then inspect an actually available later-stage route. Do not describe it as already before the issuer.

## Deliverable and checks

Return a custody chronology with who controlled each leg, what the record proves and where the chain stops. Keep contradictory tracking events visible. Ask fulfillment for missing originals; do not invent scans, signatures or carrier findings.
