# Samples and product acceptance

## When to read

A sample is available or planned, samples differ, a supplier is revising a branded item, bulk stock waiting at an agent or warehouse needs accepting, or defects require deciding what evidence supports acceptance.

## What a sample actually came from

"Can you make this?" invites the best unit anyone can find. Ask instead:

- what the supplier is producing now;
- whether this part is running on their line;
- for a sample pulled from that run.

Buyers have been sent units bought at a local retailer, and even their own earlier reference unit returned as the factory's "production sample". A unit can be perfect and still prove nothing about repeat output. A long sample delay is a question about whether that production exists, not proof of fraud: custom work takes time.

Record which kind each sample is, because each answers a different question:

- **production** sample: what the line makes now;
- **retail stock**: what someone, somewhere, made;
- **prototype**: what the design can be;
- **handmade demonstrator**: what a skilled person can make once.

An accepted reference, often called a golden sample, helps comparison. It does not define allowable variation, defect severity or the identity of hidden components.

A factory audit describes a system, a test measures a property, and an inspection checks goods against an agreed standard. Buying one is not evidence of the others.

Give each sample a local ID with its supplier, listing, variant, request revision, date, declared origin, packaging and transport route.

## Checking it

Write checks from intended use:

- dimensions at the mating surface;
- fit with accessories;
- load or function under realistic conditions;
- cleaning or repeated use where it applies;
- condition after packing.

Record the method, the observed result, its uncertainty and the acceptable range. Keep cosmetic preference separate from functional failure. If only photographs are available, mark size and function checks as unperformed. Specialist safety tests need the appropriate method and qualified interpretation.

Run each acceptance check once against a unit you know is wrong. A check that cannot fail gives false comfort: a test step can pass because it reads leftover test settings rather than the shipped state.

When a sample differs from the quote, first determine what changed: the request, the quoted offer or the supplied unit. A softer, heavier or nicer sample may be a different specification at the same listed price. Requote the configuration you prefer before choosing it. A sample can justify further investigation while remaining insufficient to approve normal customer dispatch.

Compare packaging after transport with pristine product appearance. A good unit in a protective express package does not validate lighter routine packaging.

## Revisions to branded items

For anything you asked the supplier to change, such as a printed box, an insert card, a colour or a logo, track four separate states:

1. you sent feedback;
2. you received a revised proof;
3. you approved it in writing;
4. the approved version is the one in production.

Teams lose weeks assuming the second implies the fourth. Confirm the production version against the approved proof before the first bulk shipment.

## When stock waits at an agent or warehouse

You may not control the factory, but you control what leaves the shelf. Before the first bulk order, give the agent the same written acceptance standard you would give an inspector:

- the approved sample and its version;
- the dimensions that matter;
- defect examples marked acceptable, borderline and reject;
- the packed state.

Ask the agent to check incoming cartons against that standard, with photos at a known scale.

If a received lot fails:

- **Stop outbound orders from it first.** Goods already moving cannot be sorted.
- **Compare the defect with the agreed standard.** Sometimes the failure is that the standard never reached the people producing the goods, especially through an intermediary.
- **Calibrate before sorting.** If you and the agent disagree on how widespread the problem is, draw units from several cartons. Sort them into clearly good, clearly bad and borderline, agree on the borderline pile, and only then sort the lot.
- **Treat rework as a cost.** Handling can create new damage.
- **Do not accept to fill a gap.** Accepting a bad lot because you are short of stock teaches the supplier your real standard.
- **Decide on rejected stock.** Choose what happens to rejected branded units, or they may reappear somewhere you did not intend.

A replacement shipment fixes these goods; it does not show the cause was fixed. If you actually own the design, tooling or contractual lot-release rights, read [manufacturing and batch transfer](manufacturing-and-batch-transfer.md).

## Worked decisions (illustrative)

An agent holds 240 storage tins in two colorways. A check of tins drawn from five cartons finds dented lids, all on the sand colorway, but the agent calls most of them acceptable. Hold the sand tins. Agree on what lid dent counts as borderline using photos at a known scale, then sort. Release the navy tins only once more cartons confirm the problem belongs to the sand run and not the packing.

## Deliverable and checks

A versioned [sample review](sample-review.md) with:

- comparison against the requested variant;
- observed versus reported findings;
- an acceptance or deviation decision;
- missing evidence.

For stock at an agent, add the written standard sent, the hold/release decision and the scope evidence.

Buying samples is not part of the default tool workflow.
